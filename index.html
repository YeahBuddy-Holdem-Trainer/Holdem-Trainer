How this works Reading a range chart, position, stack depth

A range chart lists every starting hand and the action to take with it in one spot. The 13 × 13 grid has pairs on the diagonal, suited hands above and to the right, and offsuit hands below and to the left. AKs sits in the top row; AKo sits in the first column.

  • Position decides width. UTG has five players left to act, so it opens about 19% of hands. The button has only the blinds behind, so it opens almost half.
  • Facing a raise, there are three choices. 3-bet your strongest hands for value, plus a few bluffs like A5s that block aces and can still make straights and flushes. Call with hands that play well after the flop. Fold the rest.
  • In tournaments, stack depth in big blinds drives everything. At 40 BB you open a little tighter than in a cash game. At 20 BB you re-shove over opens. At 15 BB and below most spots become shove or fold.
  • The table shows the action. Your seat is outlined in gold at the bottom, villain in red, folded seats are faded, and chips show what each player has put in. Action moves clockwise.

Focus mode picks the spots where you score worst more often, and about 1 hand in 5 is one you have missed before. Use the filters to drill one type of spot or one stack depth.

Tournament charts here are chip-EV approximations. Near the money and at final tables, payout pressure (ICM) makes calling ranges tighter.

How this works Painting ranges, counting combos, testing yourself

Build mode is for making your own ranges: a chart you are memorizing, a version adjusted for your game, or a guess at what a specific player does. Test yourself hides a chart and asks you to paint it from memory.

  • Painting: pick a brush, then click or drag across the grid. Starting a drag on a hand that already has that action erases instead. Pairs run down the diagonal, suited hands sit above it, offsuit hands below.
  • Combos: each pair has 6 combinations, each suited hand 4 and each offsuit hand 12, out of 1,326 total. That is why adding KTo widens a range three times as much as adding KTs.
  • Compare marks every hand where your range differs from a chart, so you can see exactly how your adjustment changes it.
  • Saved ranges stay on this device and appear in the equity calculator under "My ranges", so you can test hands against the ranges you build.
  • Test yourself scores the match on the hands either range plays, so an empty grid does not get credit for all the folds. 90% or better counts as correct in Progress.

Both coaches in your notes adjust charts to the table. Hungry Horse, for example, 3-bets wider in position against recreational openers and folds more against passive 3-bettors. Building an adjusted copy and comparing it to the chart is a good way to see those changes.

How this works Estimating preflop equity without a calculator

You cannot count outs before the flop, so players memorize a handful of matchup types instead. Name the type, recall its number, then adjust a few points for suits and connectedness.

  1. Is either hand a pair? Compare the pair to the other hand's two cards: both lower, one higher and one lower, or both higher.
  2. No pairs? Look for a shared card first (domination). Otherwise line the four cards up by rank and see whose cards are higher.
  3. Adjust. Suited adds about 2 to 3 points. Connected cards (like 76) add a point or two of straight equity. Sharing a suit with the other hand takes a little away.
Worked example: 88 vs A78 is a pair. Villain's A is higher and 7 is lower, so this is pair vs one higher, one lower. Favorite ≈ 70%, so 88 ≈ 70%. Exact: about 71%.

The cheat sheet below has every type. Learn it, then drill until you name the type in a second.

How this works The break-even formula, step by step

A call is profitable when you win often enough to pay for it. The break-even point is:

equity needed = your call ÷ (pot + villain's bet + your call)

Worked examplePot $100, villain bets $50. You call $50 to win the $150 already there. Final pot $200. $50 ÷ $200 = 25%.Ratio method: the pot offers $150 to your $50, or 3 : 1. Then 1 ÷ (3 + 1) = 25%.
  • The most common slip is dividing by the pot before your call ($50 ÷ $150 = 33%). Your own call is part of what you win back.
  • Bet sizes repeat, so memorize the table below. A half-pot bet always needs 25%, a pot bet always 33%.
  • This is the price only. Whether to call depends on comparing it with your equity, which the Call or fold trainer drills.

How this works Outs, the rule of 2 and 4, and the decision
  1. Find the price with the pot odds formula.
  2. Count your outs: the unseen cards that put you ahead. A flush draw has 9, an open-ended straight draw 8, a gutshot 4, two overcards about 6.
  3. Convert outs to equity. With two cards to come (all in on the flop), multiply by 4. With one card to come, multiply by 2.
  4. Compare. If your equity is above the price, call. If below, fold.
Worked exampleTurn. Villain bets $50 into $100, so you need 25%. You have a flush draw: 9 outs × 2 = 18%. 18% is below 25%, so fold.
  • Watch for dirty outs: a card that completes your draw but gives villain a better hand does not count. The trainer counts only clean outs.
  • Villain's cards are face up here so you can check your counting. In real play you estimate against a range; the Calculator tab handles that.
  • Not modeled: implied odds (money you win on later streets) and fold equity from raising.

How this works What equity is and how it moves street to street

Equity is your share of the pot if every remaining card were dealt with no more betting. The app deals every possible remaining board, counts how often you win, and counts a tie as half a win.

  • Preflop: use the matchup types from the Matchups tab. Name the type, recall the number.
  • Flop: if you are behind, count outs × 4. If you are ahead, count villain's outs × 4 and subtract from 100.
  • Turn: the same, × 2. Draws lose about half their value when the turn misses.
  • River: no cards to come, so equity is 100%, 50% or 0%.
  • Split pots count as half. Equity = win % + half of split %. A card that ties you with villain is worth half an out.
  • Runner-runner: when you need both the turn and the river, the rule of 4 counts nothing. These draws are usually worth 1 to 4%, so add about 1 out for each one.

After each guess the trainer shows the shortcut you could have used, so you can see whether the miss came from the method or the arithmetic.

How this works Size buckets, the two answer keys, and mistake costs

You raised before the flop and it's your turn to act. Pick a size: check, small (about ⅓ pot), medium (½–⅔), large (¾–pot), overbet (about 1.5× pot) or, when stacks are short enough, all in. The amounts in big blinds show on each button.

  • Exploit key: what the coaches in your notes do against a typical live recreational player. Most answers link to the video moment they come from.
  • GTO baseline: simplified rules of thumb for how a balanced solver strategy plays the spot. These are not solver outputs; they show the direction a balanced player leans, so you can see where the exploit departs from it.
  • You're graded on the key you pick, and you always see both after you answer.
  • Mistake cost: "best size" and "also fine" count as correct. Otherwise it's a small, medium or big mistake. Big mistakes give up clear value (checking a strong hand on the river) or put a lot of money in with the wrong hand (bluffing big into a range that just made flushes).

The four questions behind most answers: Flop: will they raise their strong hands and call with their weak ones? Turn: are they capped? Did the card change the nuts? River: what would I do with the opposite hand? If my bluff wants big, my value wants small, and the other way round.

Spots are 100 BB cash, heads-up after the flop. More spots, including facing bets, will be added over time.

How this works How a range hits a board, capped ranges, guessing first

Pick a range and a board. Every combo in the range that the board doesn't block is sorted into its best made hand (two pair, top pair, and so on). Hands weaker than two pair are also checked for draws. Percentages are of the live combos.

  • Compare two ranges to see who the board favors. The preflop raiser usually has more overpairs and top pairs on high boards; the caller often has more two pair and straights on low, connected boards.
  • Capped ranges: when a player's line removes their strongest hands (they would have raised sets and two pair on a wet flop), their "two pair or better" share shrinks. That's the "are they capped?" question from the bet-sizing trainer and your notes.
  • Turn and river: add cards to watch the numbers change. A flush card raises the flush share and turns flush draws into made hands.
  • Guess first hides the numbers until you estimate how often the range has top pair or better. Within 7.5 points counts as correct, and your average miss shows in Progress.
  • Draws are counted once, at their strongest: a flush draw plus a straight draw is a combo draw. Backdoor draws aren't counted.

How this works Opponent types, how decisions are graded, what the numbers mean

Play one heads-up hand from preflop to the river against one opponent; everyone else folds. Cash hands are 100 BB deep from the button, cutoff or big blind. Tournament hands use a big-blind ante and shorter stacks: 40 BB hands with opens and defends, 20-25 BB opens and re-shoves, and 10-15 BB push or fold. Pick the opponent type and spot, or let them be random.

  • Opponent types follow the patterns in your notes. A typical recreational player calls too much, stabs small and bets big only with strong hands. A calling station rarely folds. A nit only bets real hands. An aggressive player bluffs and raises a lot.
  • Preflop decisions are graded against the trainer's charts (cash or tournament). Calling an all-in is graded by your equity against their all-in range. Tournament values are in chips, not prize money; use the ICM tool for bubble spots.
  • After the flop, every option is scored by expected value (EV) in big blinds, one street ahead. The trainer works out the opponent's likely range from how this type played the hand so far, how often they would fold, call or raise each size, and your equity against them. The table after each decision shows the numbers.
  • Mistake cost: within about 5% of the pot of the best option counts as fine. Beyond that it's a small, medium or big mistake depending on how much of the pot it gives up.
  • One-street EV ignores later streets, so slow-plays and implied odds aren't rewarded. Treat close calls as close.

    How this works Narrowing a range street by street

    Hand reading means updating a range, not guessing one hand. Start with what they open preflop, then ask on every street: which of these hands would bet here, and which would check?

    • Bets keep strong hands, good draws and some bluffs, and drop weak pairs. Checks keep weak and medium hands and drop most strong ones.
    • Each street you estimate what share of their remaining range is top pair or better. The slider starts at the share before the action, so think about which way the action moves it.
    • After you answer, the grid shows the range re-weighted: darker hands are more likely to have played this way.
    • On the river you put them on a hand. A good read can still lose to the actual hand; what matters is the range.
    • Opponents: a solid regular, an aggressive player who bluffs more, or a tight player who bluffs less. Their opening range comes from the Cash or Tournament charts.

    The betting model is simplified: real players mix more, and sizes vary. It's built to train the habit of updating, not to match one player.

      How this works Adjusting to the player in front of you

      Balanced play makes you hard to beat. Exploiting makes the most money from players who aren't balanced: you lean away from the standard play in the direction their mistakes open up.

      • Bluff-catch: call when they bluff more than the price needs, fold when they bluff less.
      • Thin value: bet medium hands into players who call with worse; check them against players who only call with better, or who bluff when checked to.
      • Bluffs: bluff players who fold too much, never bluff calling stations.
      • C-bets: small c-bets with nothing print against players who fold to them, and burn money against players who never do.

      Each answer shows the expected value of every option against that player and what you'd do against a balanced player. Pick one of your own players from the hand log to drill against their style.

        How this works Counting combos and card removal

        Combos are the exact two-card holdings a hand stands for. Counting them tells you how likely each part of a range really is: there are three times as many AKo as AKs, and a set is often far less likely than it feels.

        • Pairs: 6 combos (4 × 3 ÷ 2). With one of that rank visible, 3. With two visible, 1.
        • Suited hands: 4 combos, one per suit. A visible card of either rank kills the suit it's in.
        • Offsuit hands: 12 combos (4 × 4 − 4 suited). With cards visible: (cards left of rank 1) × (cards left of rank 2) − suited combos left.
        • Any unpaired hand (AK with no s or o): 16 combos, or (left of rank 1) × (left of rank 2).
        • Bluff share: count value combos and bluff combos, then bluffs ÷ total. A pot-size river bet is balanced with about 1 bluff for every 2 value hands.

        How this works Range advantage, nut advantage and c-bet plans

        On the flop, think about ranges before your own hand. Three questions decide most c-bet plans:

        • Range advantage: whose whole range has more equity. High boards (A, K, Q) usually favor the preflop raiser, who has more big cards. Low, connected boards hit the caller's small pairs and suited connectors.
        • Nut advantage: who has more of the very strongest hands (two pair, sets, straights, flushes). A range can be ahead overall but short on nuts, or the other way round.
        • Texture: dry boards (rainbow, disconnected, like K-7-2) change little on later streets. Wet boards (two-tone, connected, like 9-8-6 with two hearts) have many draws.
        • Rule of thumb: range edge on a dry board, bet small with most of the range. Range and nut edge on a wet board, bet bigger with fewer hands. Caller has the edge, check a lot.

        Ranges come from the Cash or Tournament charts, so switch the game type to practise either. One spot is a 3-bet pot, where the 3-bettor's range is much stronger.

        How this works Bigger pots, more players

        3-bet pots are bigger and shallower: less money behind compared with the pot. Multiway pots have three or more players, so somebody is much more likely to have a strong hand.

        • 3-bet size: about 3× the open in position, 4× from the blinds, plus one open's worth for each caller.
        • Facing a 3-bet (cash): fold hands that play badly in a big pot, call good suited hands and pairs in position, 4-bet the best hands and a few blockers. In tournaments at 20 BB the question becomes whether to 3-bet all in.
        • SPR (stack-to-pot ratio) = money behind ÷ pot on the flop. Low SPR (about 3 or less): top pair is happy to get it in. High SPR (6+): you want two pair or better.
        • Multiway equity drops with every extra player; one-pair hands lose the most.
        • Someone has it: the more players, the likelier at least one has two pair or better. Bluff less and value bet thinner hands less.

        How this works Why tournament chips aren't money, and what the numbers mean

        In a cash game a chip is worth its face value. In a tournament it isn't: doubling your stack doesn't double your prize money, because you can only win first place once. ICM (the Independent Chip Model) turns stacks into prize money: your chance to finish first is your share of the chips, and the remaining places follow the same rule.

        • Stacks table: enter everyone still in, mark which player is you and who shoved. The results show each player's prize-money value.
        • Calling an all-in: enter the chips already in the pot (blinds and antes) and how much you have to call. The tool compares folding with calling, in prize money.
        • Equity needed with ICM is the win rate that makes calling worth as much as folding. Needed in chips only is the normal pot-odds number. The gap is the risk premium: the extra equity the payouts demand.
        • Bubble factor compares losing with winning: a bubble factor of 1.5 means losing this pot hurts you 1.5 times as much as winning it helps. It is highest near the money and when a bigger stack covers you.
        • This is why calling ranges tighten near the money and at final tables, which the preflop trainer's tournament notes mention.

        Enter stacks as the chips each player has behind right now, after blinds and antes are posted. ICM ignores skill and future hands, so treat it as a guide for big all-in decisions, not every pot.
